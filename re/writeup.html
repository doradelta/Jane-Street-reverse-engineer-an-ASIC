Reverse engineering the Jane Street ASIC

August 2026

The puzzle: a file called puzzle.gds, which is a manufacturable chip layout with every internal name stripped out, and a single goal, make the success pin go high. You get a sample waveform that does not win, a hint image, and a warmup design with all its intermediate build files so you can test your tooling.

The short version of the answer: the chip checks a Star Battle puzzle on an 11 by 11 grid. You clock in 121 bits serially, one per cycle with enable high. There is exactly one accepted input. Feed it in and success goes high, and the chip spells this on its output bus, one character per clock:

(* TWO STARS *)

Which is a valid OCaml comment, naturally.

Getting a netlist out of the geometry

The GDS keeps standard cells as named references, so the cell types are readable, but all the wiring is anonymous polygons across six routing layers. I parsed everything with gdstk and rebuilt connectivity by brute geometry: two shapes on the same layer that touch are the same electrical net, vias connect their two layers, and inside every cell the pin shapes are labeled, so a wire that lands on a pin shape is connected to that pin. Union find over all of it produces a netlist.

The warmup design is what makes this trustworthy. It ships with its own placed and routed DEF file, so I extracted my netlist from the warmup GDS and compared: all 79 cells and all 84 nets came out identical to the reference. Then I ran the same code on the real thing: 1,618 cells, 713 nets, 92 flip flops.

A small simulator

I wrote boolean functions for the 66 sky130 cell types the design uses, checked against the foundry's functional Verilog, plus flip flops with async set and reset. Combinational logic settles in topological order, the clock steps edge by edge. Replaying the provided sample waveform through it matches the recorded outputs at all 624 timestamps, bit for bit, which is what let me trust everything downstream.

What the logic turned out to be

Tracing backwards from success: it is one flip flop, set by a wide AND of pairwise conditions, all framed by a counter that repeats every 11 cycles. That counter is what turns the serial stream into rows of a grid. Part of the conditions decode into the classic Star Battle rules: two stars in every row, two in every column, and no two stars touching, diagonals included. The rest of the conditions hardwire which particular puzzle this chip was built for. That matters, because the three rules alone admit many grids, and the chip rejects every one of them except its own.

Solving it

Rather than decode the hardwired part by hand I unrolled the whole circuit over 121 clocks in z3, with the input bits symbolic, starting from the state the flops really have after reset, and asked for success = 1. It answered in under a second. Adding a clause that blocks that answer and asking again returns unsat, so the input is unique. This is the grid:

row  0   . . . . . . . * . * .     00000001010
row  1   * . . . . * . . . . .     10000100000
row  2   . . . . . . . * . * .     00000001010
row  3   * . * . . . . . . . .     10100000000
row  4   . . . . * . * . . . .     00001010000
row  5   . . * . . . . . * . .     00100000100
row  6   . . . . * . . . . . *     00001000001
row  7   . * . . . . * . . . .     01000010000
row  8   . . . * . . . . . . *     00010000001
row  9   . . . . . * . . * . .     00000100100
row 10   . * . * . . . . . . .     01010000000

The message, and the bug that hid it

The hint image marks a region as the output generator and says you can ignore it while reverse engineering. It turns out to be an 8 bit linear feedback shift register that hashes the input stream as it goes by, plus logic that turns its final value into characters. Three inputs get a message of their own: the winning grid gets (* TWO STARS *), all zeros gets EMPTY SKY, all ones gets BIG BANG. Everything else gets TRY AGAIN.

This part cost me the most time, for a silly reason. Four of the flip flops seeding that LFSR power up as 1 through an async set. A typo in my simulator meant that branch never ran, so they started at 0 and the winning grid produced garbage on the bus. Every test still passed, because the sample waveform never exercises those four flops. The fix was one line.

Checking it

Everything above can be rerun from the re folder:

python3 extract.py ../asic-puzzle-2026/puzzle.gds puzzle_netlist.json
python3 verify.py unique

The second command rechecks the grid constraints, simulates the chip on the winning grid and the easter eggs, verifies the reset state, runs the warmup netlist as an adder to confirm extraction works functionally, and redoes the z3 uniqueness proof. winning_inputs.vcd records the inputs together with the expected outputs, and replaying it through the simulator matches at all 285 timestamps.

I also put together playground.html, where the extracted netlist runs live in the browser so you can try grids against the actual circuit, and the die can be explored in 3D, layer by layer.
